Today the Church commemorates Nino of Georgia, an enslaved woman whose prayer and witness brought a royal household and a people toward the light of Christ.

In the fourth century, an enslaved Christian woman came into Iberia, the ancient kingdom now known as Georgia. Georgian Christians remember her as Nino, a woman from Cappadocia. She possessed no public office, wealth, or freedom. The people among whom she lived first noticed the pattern of her days: she prayed to Christ, fasted, and kept a chaste and sober life. When they asked what these practices meant, she answered simply that she worshiped Christ as God.

A mother brought Nino a child whom no neighbor had been able to heal. Nino said that she knew no human remedy, but that Christ could give the life for which human help had failed. She laid the child upon her rough bed, prayed, and returned the child healed to the mother.

News reached the queen, who was suffering from a grave illness. The queen sent for Nino, but the captive woman would not presume upon the royal summons. Instead, the queen had herself carried to Nino’s small cell. Nino laid her upon the same poor bed and called upon Christ. The queen rose restored to health.

The queen offered gold, silver, and fine clothing as a reward. Nino refused them. The gift she desired was that the queen should know the God who had healed her. She taught her about Jesus Christ and urged that a church be built in his honor.

The king resisted his wife’s new faith. Later, while hunting, he became separated from his companions and a great darkness closed around him. Unable to find the path, he remembered the God preached by the captive woman and vowed to worship Christ if he were delivered. Light returned, and the king found his way home.

Nino then instructed the king in the faith and showed the people how a church should be built. The royal household sent an embassy to the Emperor Constantine asking for clergy to catechize the people, baptize them, and celebrate the Eucharist. Bishops, priests, and deacons came, and the Gospel spread through the kingdom.

Georgian Christians remember Nino carrying a cross made from grapevines and bound with a strand of her hair. The cross’s drooping arms became a sign of the Church she had served. They also remember that when the king later proposed forcing mountain peoples toward baptism by the sword, Nino refused that way. God had not commanded the sword, she told him, but the proclamation of truth through the Gospel and the life-giving cross. Yet the king's men later used force against those who refused baptism.

Nino continued eastward into Kakheti, teaching and gathering people for baptism. She died at Bodbe, where her tomb became one of Georgia’s great places of pilgrimage. The Georgian Church honors her as Equal to the Apostles and Enlightener of Georgia.

The teacher that nation remembers as its enlightener arrived without rank and against her will. Nino could not command a court, yet a queen came to her cell. She could not reward the king, yet she showed him the light by which he found his way. She refused both royal payment and the sword, trusting Christ to make the witness of an enslaved woman fruitful.

With Nino, may we confess Christ through the pattern of our lives, speak the Gospel without coercion, and trust that no condition can place us beyond the reach of his calling.

## Collect

Almighty God, who called your servant Nino to be your apostle to the people of Georgia, to bring those wandering in darkness to the true light and knowledge of you: Grant us so to walk in that light, that we may come at last to the light of your everlasting day; through Jesus Christ our Lord, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, now and for ever. Amen.
