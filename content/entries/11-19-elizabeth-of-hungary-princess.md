Today the Church commemorates Elizabeth of Hungary, who recognized Jesus Christ in the poor and tended them with her own hands.

Elizabeth was born in 1207, the daughter of the King of Hungary. As a small child, she was sent to the court of Thuringia, where a marriage had been arranged for her. In 1221, at fourteen, she married Ludwig, the young ruler of the country. They loved one another and had three children.

Elizabeth rose at night to pray. Ludwig sometimes held her hand and asked her to return to rest. He supported her almsgiving, although members of the household complained about how much she gave away. Her devotion to Christ belonged within their marriage, as she prayed, cared for their children, and served the people who came to her for help.

Franciscan friars came to Thuringia and taught the life of poverty and mercy practiced by Francis of Assisi. Elizabeth listened to them. She wore simple clothing and used the wealth at her disposal to feed and clothe the poor.

In 1226, famine struck the country. While Ludwig was away, Elizabeth distributed grain and spent money to relieve those who were hungry. When he returned, his officers complained of her generosity. Ludwig allowed her to continue.

The castle at the Wartburg stood high above the town, and people weakened by illness could not climb to its gates. Elizabeth therefore established a hospital below the hill. She went there herself, made beds, brought food, and helped patients to eat. She also provided for children who had no one to care for them. At times, she sold rich clothing to obtain what the sick and hungry needed.

In 1227, Ludwig left to take part in a crusade. He died of disease in Italy that September, before reaching the Holy Land. Their third child, Gertrude, was born a few weeks after his death. In October, Elizabeth's mother-in-law brought her the news. At first, Elizabeth misunderstood and thought that Ludwig had been taken prisoner and could be brought home. Then she learned that he was dead. She cried out in grief. She was twenty years old.

Ludwig's brother expelled Elizabeth and her children from the court that winter. With two attendants, Elizabeth sought shelter in the town below the castle. Her aunt Matilda welcomed them into the monastery she governed. Sophia, Elizabeth's elder daughter, stayed there with the nuns. Elizabeth took her son and the baby to her uncle, Bishop Eckbert. He gave them a castle to live in. He urged Elizabeth to marry another ruler, but she refused.

On Good Friday in 1228, she placed her hands on the stripped altar in the church of the Franciscan friars at Eisenach. Before witnesses, she renounced the display and privilege of her former life. She wished to give away everything and beg from door to door, but Conrad of Marburg, the Priest directing her spiritual life, would not permit it. She retained resources with which she could help others.

Elizabeth went to Marburg. After a period of unsettled lodging, she built a small house and a hospice for the sick, the aged, and the poor. She lived close to the patients and served them at table. She washed people with leprosy, dressed their sores, and handled the tasks that others shrank from doing. On Maundy Thursday, she washed their feet and kissed their wounds.

Elizabeth arranged for others to care for and educate her children. Sophia had stayed with the nuns at Kitzingen. In 1229, the nuns of Altenberg took the youngest child, Gertrude, into their care.

Elizabeth had promised to obey Conrad. He removed the attendants who had known and loved her for years, including Isentrud and Guda. Elizabeth wept when they were sent away. Conrad replaced them with women who reported her smallest breaches of his commands. He struck her in the face and beat her with a rod. The marks of his blows remained for weeks.

Elizabeth continued to care for the people in the hospice. She spun and carded wool to earn money for their needs, and she worked even when she herself was ill. A nobleman sent from Hungary found the King's daughter at her spinning and offered to take her home. She chose to remain.

Prayer accompanied this labor. When Elizabeth returned from prayer, her face shone, and light radiated from her eyes. She often withdrew to pray in private before returning to the people in her care.

In November 1231, her strength gave way. As death approached, Elizabeth confessed her sins and declared that what remained of her possessions belonged to the poor. She kept only the worn dress in which she was to be buried.

She received the Body of the Lord. During the day, she spoke to those beside her about the preaching she had heard. Then she commended them to God and died quietly on November 17. She was twenty-four years old. Her body was buried in the chapel of the hospice where she had served.

The Lord whom Elizabeth had served in the hospice gave himself to her in Holy Communion. We receive him at his table, and his love sends us to the hungry and the sick.

## Collect

Almighty God, by your grace your servant Elizabeth of Hungary recognized and honored Jesus in the poor of this world: Grant that we, following her example, may with love and gladness serve those in any need or trouble, in the name and for the sake of Jesus Christ; who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever and ever. Amen.
