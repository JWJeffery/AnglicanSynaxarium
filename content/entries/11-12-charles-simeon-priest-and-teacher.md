Today the Church commemorates Charles Simeon, Priest and teacher, whose long ministry in Cambridge formed generations of clergy and whose own life was shaped by the patient work of repentance.

Charles was born at Reading in 1759 and educated at Eton before entering King’s College, Cambridge. In 1779, soon after his arrival, he learned that the college required him to receive Holy Communion. The prospect frightened him. He knew how little his life had been directed toward God, and he could not treat the sacrament as an empty university observance. He began to pray, examine himself, and seek help in books of devotion. He received Communion a few weeks later, but his distress remained.

As Easter approached and he prepared to receive again, he read Bishop Thomas Wilson’s instructions for receiving the Lord’s Supper. A passage on sacrifice opened to him the possibility of entrusting his guilt to Christ. Hope grew over the following days. On Easter morning he awoke with the words “Jesus Christ is risen today” upon his lips. At Easter Communion in the college chapel, he found peace in the mercy of God. He remembered that Easter throughout his life.

In 1782, while still a deacon, Simeon was appointed to Holy Trinity Church in Cambridge. He had often passed the church and wished that he might preach there. When the incumbent died, he asked his father to approach the Bishop of Ely on his behalf. The parishioners wanted their existing curate to receive the appointment. The Bishop chose Simeon, and the young minister entered a parish already bitterly opposed to him. He was ordained Priest the following year.

The opposition took a form that could be seen every Sunday. Those who controlled the enclosed pews locked them and stayed away, leaving others unable to use the seats. People who wished to hear Simeon crowded into the aisles. He paid for benches and open seating in the available spaces, but the churchwardens removed them. Many parishioners would not receive him in their houses. When he sought to add an evening service, the church doors were locked against him.

Simeon found a room where he could meet his people, open the Scriptures with them, and pray. He sought counsel from older clergy and continued the work before him. A sentence from Saint Paul held his anger in check: “The servant of the Lord must not strive.” He prayed that those who did come might receive an abundant blessing, and he stayed. His ministry at Holy Trinity would last fifty-four years.

He belonged to the evangelical movement within the Church of England. Its emphasis on conversion, Scripture, and the saving work of Christ marked his preaching. He wanted the people who heard the familiar words of Christian worship to offer their lives to God. The experience that had brought him to this conviction had taken place in preparation for Communion, and he remained devoted to the Church’s ministry and common prayer. In 1811, he preached four sermons before the University of Cambridge on The Excellency of the Liturgy, giving public expression to his enduring love of the Church’s common prayer.

His pastoral care reached beyond the pulpit. In 1783, while waiting to conduct a burial in a London churchyard, he read an epitaph expressing trust in Christ’s saving death. Seeing a young woman reading the gravestones nearby, he called her over and invited her to consider its words. She was exhausted by the effort to support her elderly mother and two children. Her health had failed, and she could no longer provide what they needed.

Simeon asked for her address and visited the following evening. He found the mother ill and the household overwhelmed by distress. He knelt and prayed with them, struggling through tears to speak. He returned on the next evening, and again on the third. The young woman then told him that, when he had first spoken to her in the churchyard, she had been on the point of taking her own life. Through their meeting and his visits, she had begun to hope again.

The family received continuing help. A weekly allowance was provided, and the children were cared for as they grew. Years later, Simeon said that this one encounter would have been sufficient recompense for the labor of his whole life.

Patience remained a discipline he had to learn. Small frustrations could bring out a sharp temper. At a dinner, he struck a servant on the back to stop him tending the fire in a way he disliked. During the same visit, he lost his temper over the wrong bridle being put on his horse. A friend who had witnessed his behavior rebuked him in a letter written as though it came from a servant. Simeon replied with thanks, acknowledged the correction as deserved, and asked to be corrected again if he failed to improve. He signed his answer, “Chas. Proud and Irritable.” In another letter he admitted how readily he fell back into the same faults when he ceased to watch and pray. The preacher who called others to repentance submitted his own life to it.

As the years passed, students and younger clergy gathered around him. In his rooms at King’s, they brought questions that had arisen in their reading and in the work of ministry. At his Friday evening gatherings, benches were set out for the visitors. Simeon welcomed newcomers, learned their names and colleges, and entered the details in a small notebook. Their questions gave him occasions to teach, encourage, and correct.

He also helped them prepare to preach. He taught them to attend closely to a passage of Scripture, discover its meaning, and let its character shape the sermon. His published outlines placed the fruit of years of study at the service of other ministers. He resisted the habit of making every biblical text repeat a favorite party doctrine. The preacher had to remain teachable before the breadth of Scripture.

His concern for the Church extended beyond Cambridge. He helped to found the Church Missionary Society and encouraged people called to serve overseas. Among those he supported was Henry Martyn, whose vocation joined preaching with the patient work of learning languages and translating the Scriptures. Simeon also gave sustained support to the London Society for Promoting Christianity Amongst the Jews. He sought the conversion of Jewish people to Christianity, preached and raised funds for this work, and traveled on its behalf. Simeon’s rooms and parish became places from which others were strengthened to undertake their own work.

Simeon died in Cambridge on November 13, 1836, and was buried at King’s College. He had remained in the parish that once shut its doors against him, receiving correction, serving his people, and teaching others to serve. Through those long years, the grace he had encountered in Communion worked upon his habits and affections. His perseverance brings us to ask for that same grace: to walk with Christ, receive correction, and grow in the patient love by which he makes his people holy.

## Collect

Loving God, whose unerring wisdom and unbounded love order all things: Grant us in all things to see your hand; that, following the example and teaching of your servant Charles Simeon, we may walk with Christ in all simplicity and serve you with a quiet and contented mind; through Jesus Christ our Lord, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever and ever. Amen.
