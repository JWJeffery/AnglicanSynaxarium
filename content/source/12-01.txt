**Charles de Foucauld, Monastic and Witness — December 1st**

Today the Church commemorates Charles de Foucauld, monk, priest, and martyr, whose search for God led him into the Sahara, where he sought to live as a brother among people whose faith was not his own.

Charles was born in Strasbourg in 1858. After his parents died when he was six, his maternal grandfather took Charles and his younger sister into his home. As a young man, he abandoned the Christian faith in which he had been raised. He entered the French army, but military discipline did little to govern his conduct. His early adult years were marked by indulgence and restlessness.

After serving in North Africa, Charles resigned his commission and undertook a difficult journey through Morocco. There he encountered Muslims whose disciplined prayer impressed him deeply. Their reverence for God recalled him to the question of faith. Back in France, he sought counsel from a priest, Abbé Henri Huvelin. In 1886 he returned to confession and Holy Communion. The change reached further than his religious observance. He began to ask how his whole life might belong to Christ.

Charles made a pilgrimage to the Holy Land and was drawn especially to Nazareth, where Jesus had lived for years in the obscurity of ordinary work. He entered a Trappist monastery and spent seven years in a life of prayer, fasting, labor, and obedience, first in France and then in Syria. Yet he continued to seek a form of poverty closer to the hidden life of Jesus. After leaving the Trappists, he lived in Nazareth as a servant of the Poor Clares, occupying a small dwelling near their convent and spending long hours in prayer before the Blessed Sacrament.

The sisters encouraged him toward the priesthood. Charles was ordained in 1901 and returned to the Sahara, first to Béni Abbès and later to Tamanrasset, among the Tuareg people of the Hoggar. He hoped to make his life an offering for those he called the most abandoned. He wanted to live among them as a brother, not a conqueror. Charles sought, as he put it, “to shout the Gospel with his life.” He hoped to live in such a way that others might ask, “If such is the servant, what must the Master be like?”

He did not abandon his desire that his neighbors should know Christ. Yet he did not make friendship depend on conversion. At Béni Abbès in 1903, Charles baptized a gravely ill woman named Marie, who had asked him to receive her into the Church. Later, among the Tuareg, he visited his neighbors, offered food and help, and learned their language. With Tuareg collaborators he gathered their poetry and sayings and labored over dictionaries and translations.

Charles traveled into Tuareg country with French military expeditions, including one led by his friend Henri Laperrine. He remained closely connected to French officers and defended aspects of France's rule in North Africa. His friendships with Tuareg neighbors endured alongside those commitments.

During a terrible drought, food grew scarce across the Hoggar. Charles shared his provisions until he had too little for himself. In January 1908, weakened by hunger and illness, he became unable to work and feared that he was dying. Then his Tuareg friends searched for goats that could still give milk. They brought what little they found to the sick priest. He had brought food to his neighbors. Now they brought him what they could spare.

In Tamanrasset, Charles saw no conversions among the Tuareg. He continued to celebrate Mass, pray, study, receive visitors, and prepare a rule for companions who might one day share his life. None joined him in the desert.

In 1916, the war spreading across Europe had reached the Sahara. Armed men attacked his hermitage at Tamanrasset on December 1. Charles was seized and shot to death. He died without the religious community he had hoped to establish and without seeing the outward success by which missionaries were often measured.

After his death, men and women took up the pattern of life he had described: prayer before Christ, simplicity of living, and friendship with those among whom they dwelt. In 1908, the neighbors he hoped to serve had fed him when he could no longer feed himself. In this mutual care, his longing to live as a brother found its earthly form. May God give us the grace to seek Christ among our neighbors, to receive their kindness, and to serve them faithfully even when we cannot see the fruit of our labor.

Let us pray.

Loving God, help us to know you wherever we find you and to serve you in all people, that with your servant Charles de Foucauld we may be faithful even unto death; through Jesus Christ, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, now and for ever. Amen.
