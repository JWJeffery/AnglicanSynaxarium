**John of Damascus, Priest and Theologian — December 4th**

Today the Church commemorates John of Damascus, priest, monk, and theologian, who defended the holy images because the eternal Son of God had truly taken human flesh.

John was born into a Christian family in Damascus during the seventh century. The city had come under Muslim rule, but Christians continued to live, worship, and work there. His father held an important position in the caliph's administration, and John received an education in Scripture, theology, philosophy, and the learning of the ancient world. John also served in the caliph's government.

About 715, John left his government position and entered the monastery of Saint Sabas, in the wilderness near Jerusalem. There he joined men whose days were ordered by the psalms, prayer, labor, and the common life. He was ordained a priest. In his cell and among his fellow monks, he continued the study of the faith he had learned in Damascus.

In the Byzantine Empire, Emperor Leo III moved against the veneration of holy images, and opposition to their use spread under imperial authority. John lived outside the emperor's territory, under the Muslim caliph, and could answer those commands without submitting to the emperor's jurisdiction. Around this time, he wrote three treatises defending the images of Christ and the saints.

John's defense began with the Incarnation. God in his divine nature cannot be painted. Yet the eternal Word became human, born of the Virgin Mary. Jesus could be seen, held, and touched; he suffered, died, and rose in the flesh he had taken for our salvation. John therefore defended images of Christ as a confession that the Son of God had truly entered the visible world.

John also distinguished the worship due to God alone from the honor given before a holy image. “I do not worship matter; I worship the Creator of matter who became matter for my sake,” he wrote. The body was not beneath God's concern: the Son had taken human nature to heal it and bring human beings into communion with himself.

At Saint Sabas, John gathered the teaching of earlier fathers into The Fount of Knowledge. He set out the Church's faith concerning the Trinity, Christ, and the sacraments in a form that later generations could study.

He also wrote hymns for the Church's feasts. At Easter, the words of his Paschal praise proclaimed the victory of the risen Lord over death. English translations of his songs later entered Anglican hymnody, including “Come, ye faithful, raise the strain” and “The day of resurrection.” What he taught in his books, the people of God could sing in worship.

John died at Saint Sabas in the eighth century. His defense of images endured through years of controversy. The Seventh Ecumenical Council, meeting at Nicaea in 787, affirmed that honor shown to a holy image passes to the one depicted, while worship belongs to God alone. John had joined his pen and his voice to the confession of the incarnate and risen Lord. May God confirm us in that same faith and bring us, with John, to the Easter joy his hymns proclaim.

Let us pray.

Confirm our minds, O Lord, in the mysteries of the true faith, set forth with power by your servant John of Damascus; that we, with him, confessing Jesus to be true God and true Man and singing the praises of the risen Lord, may, by the power of the resurrection, attain to eternal joy; through Jesus Christ our Lord, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, now and for ever. Amen.
