# Sojourner Truth

## Preacher and Abolitionist, 1883 — November 26

Today the Church commemorates Sojourner Truth, who received Christ's mercy amid slavery and suffering and carried the Gospel into her work for the freedom of others.

She was born about 1797 in Ulster County, New York, and was named Isabella Baumfree. Her parents were enslaved, and Dutch was her first language. Her mother had seen older children sold away. In the evenings she taught the children still with her to pray to God, who could hear them wherever their owners sent them.

At about nine years old, Isabella was sold away from her parents. Her new owner beat her. After further sales she came into the household of John Dumont, who held her in slavery for many years. She endured sexual abuse in that household. She later married Thomas, another enslaved man. The people who owned them could separate parents and children at will.

New York was preparing to end slavery for adults born before 1799. Dumont promised Isabella an earlier release, then withheld it. In 1826 she left his house before dawn, carrying her infant daughter, Sophia. She could not take her older children with her. Thomas remained with them at Dumont's farm. Isaac and Maria Van Wagenen received mother and child and paid Dumont for Isabella's remaining service so that he could not force her back.

During her stay with them, Isabella learnt that her young son Peter had been sold out of New York and taken to Alabama. The sale was unlawful. She went to seek his return, though she had little money and no powerful connections. She asked God to make the people who held authority listen to her. Neighbors and lawyers helped her to bring the case before a court.

In those same months, a holiday approached and Isabella longed to join the celebrations with her former companions. Dumont came to the Van Wagenens' home. Isabella was preparing to leave with him when the presence of God overwhelmed her. She remembered promises she had made to God and broken. She wanted to pray, but could find no words. Then she saw someone standing between her and the God she feared. His love was familiar, though she could not name him. At last she knew that it was Jesus. He had loved her before she had known him. Her terror gave way to joy.

In 1828 the court ordered Peter's return. The following year Isabella and her son moved to New York City. She worked in other people's homes and worshiped with an African Methodist congregation.

She also entered a household that came under the control of Robert Matthews, who called himself Matthias and claimed divine authority. She trusted him for a time and lost the savings she had put into the community. When a man in the household died, other members accused Isabella of poisoning him. She defended herself, cleared her name, and obtained compensation for slander. She gradually recognized the deception in which she had shared.

Isabella returned to earning her living. Sometimes an employer gave her money to hire a poor man to clear snow, and she did the work herself and kept the payment. When a man protested that he needed the job for his family, she answered that she was poor too. Later she remembered those exchanges and the Lord's command to love her neighbor. She saw the need of the people whose work she had taken.

Peter went to sea in 1839. Letters reached her for a time, but when his ship returned in 1842 he was absent. She never heard from him again.

On June 1, 1843, Isabella left New York to preach. She took the name Sojourner Truth and set out towards Long Island with a small bundle and little money. She spoke of Jesus, called people to turn from sin, and told them what slavery had done to families. When she needed rest, she worked in households that received her. She traveled to religious meetings, spoke, and sang.

In Massachusetts she joined a community committed to ending slavery and met people who helped her reach larger audiences. She never learnt to read, but listened to the Scriptures and held their words in her memory. She dictated the story of her life to Olive Gilbert and sold the book to support herself.

In 1849 she visited her daughter Diana and met Dumont again. He had once regarded enslaved people as property. Now he confessed that slavery had been wicked. Sojourner rejoiced to hear him say it. She called her former master a brother and asked the Lord to bless him.

At a women's rights convention in Akron, Ohio, in 1851, she spoke from her own labor and from the Gospel. She recalled Mary and Martha asking Jesus to raise their brother Lazarus. Christ had heard the women who came to him in faith and love. She would not accept an account of Christian life that silenced women whom the Lord received.

In 1857 Sojourner moved to Harmonia in Michigan, a community of spiritualists who sought communication with the dead. From there she continued to travel and speak against slavery. During the Civil War, Sojourner helped recruit Black men for the Union Army. She also worked among formerly enslaved people in Washington, seeking food, shelter, and a secure place for them to live. After the war she continued to press for land and livelihoods for people whose legal freedom had left them destitute.

In later years she lived in Battle Creek, Michigan. As her health declined she continued to travel and speak. She died at home on November 26, 1883. For Sojourner, the slaveholder had become a brother. Christ's mercy had reached across a division that law and force had maintained. In that mercy, the Church finds courage to speak and room to receive those whose pain has been ignored.

Let us pray.

O God, whose Spirit guides us into all truth and makes us free: Strengthen and sustain us as you did your servant Sojourner. Give us vision and courage to stand against oppression and injustice and all that works against the glorious liberty to which you call all your children; through Jesus Christ our Savior, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever and ever. Amen.
