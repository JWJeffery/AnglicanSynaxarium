**Richard Rolle, Walter Hilton, and Margery Kempe, Mystics — November 9th**

Today the Church commemorates Richard Rolle, Walter Hilton, and Margery Kempe, whose lives and writings helped generations of English Christians seek union with God.

Richard Rolle was born in Yorkshire around 1300. A patron enabled him to study at Oxford, but at about eighteen he left the university and returned to northern England to become a hermit. He asked his sister for two of her tunics. From these and his father’s hood he fashioned a hermit’s habit. When she saw what he had done, she cried out that her brother had gone mad. Fearing that his family would seize him, Richard fled.

Richard experienced the love of God as heat, sweetness, and song. While praying, he felt a warmth so vivid that he wondered whether it came from a bodily cause. As his prayer deepened, he knew an inward sweetness and a heavenly melody that transformed his recitation of the psalms. In *The Fire of Love*, he sought words for this encounter with God, inviting others to turn their desires toward Jesus.

He wrote in Latin and in English, giving instruction to people who sought his counsel. For the anchoress Margaret Kirkby, who had withdrawn into a cell for a life of prayer, he wrote *The Form of Living*. His English commentary on the Psalter also joined the words of Scripture to the practice of devotion. His teaching reached readers who could not have followed his Latin works.

In his final years, Richard lived near the Cistercian convent at Hampole in South Yorkshire and served as a spiritual guide to its nuns. He died in 1349. During the following century, his writings became some of the most widely read works of devotion in England.

Walter Hilton was born around 1340 and probably studied at Cambridge. He spent a period as a hermit before entering Thurgarton Priory in Nottinghamshire as an Augustinian canon, sharing in its common life of prayer.

In *The Scale of Perfection*, Walter described the Christian life as the restoration of God’s image in the soul. Sin had disfigured that image through pride, anger, envy, and disordered desire. Through the grace of Jesus, the person was to be formed anew in humility and charity. Contemplation belonged to this transformation: the soul learned to know and love God as Christ purified its loves.

Walter warned his readers against mistaking bodily heat, sweetness, or beautiful sounds for contemplation itself. Such experiences might accompany grace, but they were not its measure. Spiritual knowledge and love belonged to the soul, and their fruit appeared in charity. Those who lacked extraordinary consolations could still persevere in the faith of the Church and live by faith working through love.

To a man responsible for lands and other people, Walter wrote an account of the mixed life of prayer and service. He warned him against neglecting those dependent upon him in order to spend all his time in devotion. To honor Christ’s head while leaving his members naked and uncared for was no honor to Christ. The man should attend to his household and those who needed his help, then return to prayer. The love of God bound him to both.

Walter died in 1396. His writings continued to guide Christians through the centuries, and later Anglican readers found in him a teacher of the contemplative life rooted in the Church’s faith and sacramental worship.

Margery Kempe was born around 1373 in the Norfolk port now called King’s Lynn. She married John Kempe and bore at least fourteen children. Her life of prayer unfolded amid the demands of marriage and a household.

After the birth of her first child, Margery became gravely ill and sent for a priest. She wanted to confess a sin she had long concealed, but his rebuke frightened her before she could speak of it. A prolonged crisis followed in which she despaired and could scarcely be restrained from harming herself. Then Christ appeared beside her bed and said, “Daughter, why have you forsaken me, and I never forsook you?” His mercy restored her hope.

After her recovery, Margery remained proud of her fine clothes and eager to prosper. She opened a brewery, but the brewing failed. She then tried a horse-mill for grinding grain, and that venture failed too. As she turned more fully to prayer, she contemplated the humanity of Jesus: his birth, his suffering, and the sorrow of his mother. Her prayer moved her to tears, sometimes to loud weeping that interrupted worship. She prayed earnestly for sinners and grieved over the Passion of Christ.

Margery also sought a life of chastity within her marriage. On a hot Midsummer Eve, as she and John returned from the direction of York, they stopped beside a cross. John set conditions for their agreement, including payment of his debts before her pilgrimage and her sharing his Friday meals. Margery prayed, and they reached an agreement.

She sought counsel from Julian of Norwich, who taught her to test her experiences by their fruit: whether they honored God and increased charity toward her fellow Christians. Margery traveled to Canterbury, Jerusalem, Rome, and Santiago de Compostela. Her public religious speech brought accusations of Lollardy, a movement condemned for rejecting aspects of the Church’s teaching and authority. At York she was examined before Archbishop Henry Bowet and answered questions about her belief. He found no heresy in her answers.

For seven or eight years, a priest read works of devotion aloud to Margery. Among them were Walter Hilton’s book and Richard Rolle’s *Fire of Love*. The hermit’s and the canon’s words thus entered the prayer of a married woman who would later dictate her own account.

Later, when John became disabled after a fall, Margery feared that caring for him would hinder her contemplation. Christ directed her to receive him and serve him for his sake. She brought John home and nursed him through his remaining years, washing his soiled clothes and attending to his bodily needs. The work was burdensome, and she struggled with it. Yet she learned to serve her husband as she would have served Christ himself.

Margery dictated what became *The Book of Margery Kempe*, preserving her pilgrimages, revelations, difficulties, and growth in compassion. She lived into the late 1430s; the date of her death is unknown.

The books read aloud to Margery taught her to seek the love of Jesus. In the care of John, that love required hands willing to wash and tend him. May Christ form his likeness in us through prayer, and give us patience with the bodily needs of those entrusted to our care.

Let us pray.

Direct our hearts, O gracious God, and inspire our minds; that like your servants Richard Rolle, Walter Hilton, and Margery Kempe, we may be purified in love, and at last behold your glory face to face; in the Name of Jesus Christ our Lord; who with you and the Holy Spirit lives and reigns, one God, for ever and ever. Amen.
