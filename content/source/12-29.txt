# Thomas Becket

## Archbishop of Canterbury and Martyr, 1170 — December 29

Today the Church commemorates Thomas Becket, archbishop of Canterbury and martyr, who died in his cathedral for the name of Jesus and the freedom of the Church.

Thomas was born in London to a Norman family. Education in England and France prepared him for service in the household of Archbishop Theobald of Canterbury. Theobald sent him to study law. Thomas became a deacon and then archdeacon of Canterbury, the archbishop's senior administrative officer.

His ability brought him to the notice of Henry II. In 1155 the king made him chancellor, one of the highest officers of the realm. Thomas became Henry's close companion and a vigorous servant of royal power. He entertained lavishly, traveled in splendor, and led troops on the king's campaigns. He also gave generously to the poor.

When Theobald died, Henry secured Thomas's election as archbishop of Canterbury. The king expected his friend to continue supporting the interests of the Crown. Thomas foresaw that his duty as archbishop could conflict with his service to the king.

He received priestly ordination and episcopal consecration in 1162 and resigned the chancellorship. As archbishop he fasted, kept vigils, and washed the feet of the poor, though his household still displayed the magnificence of a great lord. He remained forceful and often inflexible in pursuing what he believed his office required.

Henry maintained that he was restoring customs exercised by earlier kings. Thomas believed that those customs subjected the Church's judgments too fully to the Crown. Their disputes concerned taxation, the trial of clergy in royal courts, and appeals to Rome. At Clarendon in 1164, Henry set out sixteen statements of the customs he claimed. Thomas first assented to them, then withdrew his agreement.

The breach deepened. Henry demanded a full account of Thomas's years as chancellor and claimed that Thomas owed money to the Crown. Thomas resisted the royal claims and appealed to the pope. He escaped to France, where he remained in exile for six years.

At Pontigny and later at Sens, Thomas continued to defend his cause. He excommunicated some royal supporters, formally excluding them from communion. Henry took control of Thomas's lands and banished the archbishop's relatives from England. Neither man readily gave way, and repeated negotiations failed to restore a lasting peace.

In 1170 they reached a fragile reconciliation, and Thomas returned to Canterbury. During his absence, other bishops had crowned Henry's son, a rite Canterbury claimed as its own. The pope and Thomas imposed Church penalties on the bishops involved, barring some from ministry and others from communion. Those penalties brought the quarrel to its final crisis.

Henry denounced Thomas in anger. Four knights who heard him went to Canterbury. On December 29, they confronted the archbishop and demanded submission. When the encounter grew dangerous, the monks took Thomas into the cathedral, where they had begun the evening service.

The knights followed with drawn swords. The monks tried to bar the doors, but Thomas ordered them opened. He said that a house of prayer must not be made a fortress.

The armed men demanded that Thomas restore the bishops to communion and office. He refused to reverse the penalties without repentance. He declared himself ready to die but commanded the knights not to harm his companions.

When the knights tried to drag him away, Thomas resisted and pushed one of them back. Then he commended himself and the Church to God, to the Blessed Virgin Mary, and to Saint Denis, the patron of France.

Edward Grim, a clerk who remained beside him, raised his arm to ward off a sword stroke. The blade wounded Grim and struck the archbishop's head. Further blows brought Thomas to his knees. He said, “For the name of Jesus and the protection of the church I am ready to embrace death.” The attackers split his skull, and he died on the cathedral floor.

The monks prepared his body for burial. Beneath the splendid outer garments of an archbishop, they found the coarse hair shirt he had worn secretly in penance.

People soon came to pray at his tomb, and the Church received him as a martyr. Henry later came to Canterbury, prayed at Thomas's tomb, asked the monks for pardon, and allowed each monk to strike him with a rod as an act of penance.

Grace turned the splendid servant of royal power into a penitent bishop who accepted death rather than abandon the people and freedom entrusted to him. May Christ make us steadfast in repentance, courageous in witness, and faithful in the care of his Church.

Let us pray.

O God, our strength and our salvation, you called your servant Thomas Becket to be a shepherd of your people and a defender of your church; Keep your household from all evil and raise up faithful pastors and leaders who are wise in the ways of the gospel; through Jesus Christ, the shepherd of our souls, who lives and reigns with you and the Holy Spirit, one God, for ever and ever. Amen.
